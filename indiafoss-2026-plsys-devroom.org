** Summary
GiGi described an experiment in evicting KV cache entries without ever looking at the attention matrix. The motivation is running open models on a laptop: the weights take up VRAM, the KV cache grows with every token, and you hit OOM. Well-known eviction policies like H2O and SnapKV choose which tokens to keep using attention scores. That needs the full n×n attention matrix, and modern kernels such as FlashAttention, PyTorch SDPA and vLLM's PagedAttention never write that matrix out.

The idea is to score tokens using only the key and value tensors already in the cache. A small key norm tends to mean the token gets more attention after the softmax, and a large value norm means the token contributes more to the next token. That gives a "value-weighted norm score" per token, with a γ weighting term and an ε to avoid dividing by zero. It's paired with a layer-level policy, LARA (Layer-Adaptive Recency Allocation). Early layers and recent layers matter, middle layers are mostly redundant, so the middle layers can be compressed harder. The talk also included a bug story: chunked sweeps over a 32k context kept overwriting earlier chunks until a cyclic tracking scheme was added.

Results came from an 8 GB RTX 5060 laptop. H2O and SnapKV crashed on downstream QA. The attentionless policy ("EntropyKV") was reported as about 32× faster, and with 50% of the KV cache evicted it still found the needle in a needle-in-a-haystack test. The analogy he used was JPEG: you lose fine detail but keep what the image means.

** Summary
Anupam (Arista Networks, Functional Programming India) opened with a promise: by the end of the talk you'd love strong static types. He organised the talk around the two usual complaints, that types are rigid ("how do I tell the compiler what I already know?") and brittle ("one change ripples into a hundred places"). His answer was to make types stronger rather than looser. A rich type system and a strict compiler go together. You declare a type once and use it many times, so the compiler does the bookkeeping for you. He also took a jab at Rust's borrow checker, calling the lifetime annotations a sign that the type system isn't expressive enough.

The techniques, in Haskell and PureScript:
- Make invalid states unrepresentable. A record with a =Maybe= warning and a =Maybe= result allows "neither", which can't actually happen. The =These a b= sum type allows only this, that, or both. Its =Applicative= instance, with warnings collected through a =Semigroup=, lets you combine a port check and a host check with no boilerplate.
- Parse, don't validate. A function's output should carry more structure than its input: =NonEmpty= instead of checking for empty again, a set after deduplicating, a sorted array after sorting. Functions can also ask for structure up front, as in =divide :: Int -> NonZeroInt -> Int=.
- Phantom types and provenance. Use =Map r k a= with keys tagged by the same =r=, so a lookup can't fail and there's no runtime cost. He pointed to /Ghosts of Departed Proofs/ for the general form (for example, proving that two sorted lists were sorted with the same comparator before merging them).
- Ways to build stronger types: structural tricks (a non-empty array is a head plus a tail), smart constructors with the raw constructor hidden, and phantom types.
- Structural flexibility: PureScript row types (with constraints like =Lacks "createdAt"=), polymorphic variants for open error sets, and optics (=view=, =over=, =set=) to get rid of hand-written pattern matching.

In Q&A he said these patterns can be copied in C or JavaScript through discipline, but nothing enforces them. On AI, he argued that strong types suit generate-until-it-compiles loops. Rust is currently easier for LLMs to produce than readable Haskell, though.

** Summary
Agnish (Nimbus, an Ethereum consensus client written in Nim) explained PeerDAS, which stands for Peer Data Availability Sampling. He started with familiar examples of erasure coding: scratched CDs and half-torn UPI QR codes that still scan. Ethereum does the same thing with Reed–Solomon-style coding. Data bytes become points on a polynomial over a prime field of about 2^255, the polynomial is evaluated at extra parity points, and any half of the extended points is enough to rebuild the original. His toy example encoded "FOSS".

Peers in a P2P network can misbehave in two ways: they can lie (send fake data) or hide it (withhold data). Hashing doesn't help with the first, because garbage can be hashed too. Lying is handled with cryptographic commitments and proofs over the extended data, so a small piece can be checked against the commitment. Hiding is handled by sampling.

Background: since 2024 Ethereum has carried "blobs", data posted by L2 rollups that want Ethereum's security. Demand grew from 6 blobs per block toward 14, which put pressure on every node's resources. PeerDAS extends each blob into 128 columns made of cells with proofs. An attacker has to withhold more than half the columns to make data unrecoverable, so a node that checks 8 random columns is fooled about 1 time in 256, 16 columns gives 1 in 65,536, and around 30 gives about 1 in a billion. Each node holds custody of at least 4 columns, chosen from its node ID, and fetches them over libp2p gossip subnets. Nodes that hold 64 or more columns reconstruct all 128 and re-gossip them.

Effect: a sampling node stores 1/8 of the data (1/16 at minimum custody). With 14 blobs, bandwidth falls from about 1.8 MB to about 224 KB per slot, so a 15–20 Mbps line can run a node. The trade-off is that large institutional stakers end up doing most of the reconstruction. Future work splits data into smaller cells to remove the "need half the columns" threshold.

** Summary
Navaneeth (FP Launchpad, IIT Madras) gave the talk in two halves: why benchmarking is hard, and what the OCaml community is building to deal with it.

For the first half he used a long-running Java PDF pipeline. Run it several times and the numbers drift: JIT warm-up, GC behaviour, P-cores versus E-cores, thermal and power throttling, ASLR, background updates, even the season (February runs cooler than July). Some noise is periodic and some is random. His points were that performance is a distribution, not a number, that you control what you can and collect evidence for the rest, and that sometimes you throw everything out and start again.

The second half covered OCaml 5, which added multicore parallelism and effect-based concurrency. The old benchmark suite drifted away from real workloads: the compiler and runtime got tuned for the benchmarks while production code, such as Jane Street's adoption of OCaml 5, didn't get faster. The new work has two parts:
- A benchmarking service built on macro-benchmarks drawn from long-lived real programs (bioinformatics, parsers, static analysers). It uses a reproducible harness on perf/DTrace with opam sandboxes, so contributors can see improvements and regressions on their PRs.
- An evaluation of the suite itself. They define experiments with an orchestrator and use PCA and k-means to check whether the benchmarks cover enough distinct behaviour, to trim redundant ones, and to spot gaps. That analysis found too few multi-domain and long-running workloads.

The payoff is upstreaming GC and compaction work with confidence. In Q&A he mentioned his own automatic-compaction PR. The tooling showed his machine was the problem and exposed alignment effects, which he fixed with compiler flags, and it showed compaction causes no slowdown beyond error bounds.

** Summary
Bhuvan (Point Blank, ACM SIGPLAN mentee) walked through what LLVM does differently when the target is a GPU. He started with a plain =if= on =threadIdx=. On a CPU each thread takes one branch. On a GPU a warp (32 threads, or a wavefront on AMD) executes in lockstep, so a divergent branch effectively runs both sides one after the other.

Main points:
- The middle end is shared, but the target decides. The pass manager asks =TargetTransformInfo= (TTI) whether an optimisation pays off for the target, so the same IR gets different treatment. For example, a SAXPY loop gets vectorised for x86.
- Address spaces. GPUs expose global (HBM/VRAM), shared (on-die, low latency, managed by the programmer), local and constant memory. LLVM records this in the pointer type and uses it during instruction selection.
- Uniformity/divergence analysis, developed over several years (2015, 2018, 2022). Values derived from per-lane IDs are tracked through data flow, control flow and phi nodes, and marked divergent or uniform. On x86 everything comes out uniform.
- =StructurizeCFG= (AMDGPU) uses that analysis to restructure divergent control flow, so the hardware can mask lanes and run then-branch and else-branch one after the other with an explicit reconvergence point. For NVPTX, reconvergence is mostly left to =ptxas=.
- Register allocation and occupancy. GPUs have tens of thousands of registers. The aim isn't keeping hot values in registers so much as leaving room for enough resident warps that the scheduler can hide latency.

He closed by pointing out that Triton, CUDA (NVVM is an LLVM fork), HIP and Julia all build on LLVM, so learning LLVM's GPU path is the way into contributing to them.

The stack:
- AcceleratedKernels.jl: portable parallel algorithms (sort, reduce, mapreduce, accumulate, foreachindex) with target-specific tuning. Most of his GSoC work went here. He walked through accumulate (prefix scan): a local-memory scan per work group, then a block-level scan, then a final pass.
- KernelAbstractions.jl: a portable kernel model. =@kernel=, =@index(Global/Local)=, =@localmem= and =@synchronize= lift vendor-specific primitives like =threadIdx= to a common level.
- GPUArrays.jl: an abstract device array behind =CuArray=, =ROCArray= and the rest.
- KernelInterface: device facts such as maximum work-group size (1024 on CUDA, 256 on Metal), because "portable does not mean hardware-blind".
- GPUCompiler.jl: lowers Julia typed IR to LLVM IR and from there to PTX, AMDGCN/HSA or SPIR-V.
- Backend packages (CUDA.jl, AMDGPU.jl, oneAPI.jl, Metal.jl) handle what can't be abstracted, such as native memory fences.

Benchmarks on AMD: AcceleratedKernels' accumulate is within about 2–2.4% of rocThrust, and its reverse kernel is slightly faster. Trade-offs: the ecosystem is young (APIs and compiler internals still move), and hand-tuned vendor libraries start ahead. In Q&A he said, compared with Mojo, that the point is serving Julia's scientific-computing ecosystem, that mixed Nvidia/AMD clusters are possible, and that anything written in Julia, including a hypothetical JAX-like library, gets all four backends.

** Summary
This was the closing talk. Afnan described himself as a BTech dropout and startup founder who came from AI and web development. A friend showed him Verilog, he decided to write his own and was promptly humbled, and Frag came out of that: a small Rust-based HDL compiler. He had forgotten his laptop and the borrowed one wouldn't connect over HDMI, so the talk ran without a demo.

Main points:
- Hardware compilers need a semantic analysis stage on top of parsing. Signal widths, clocks and timing matter, and in hardware everything happens at once, not step by step.
- Frag's pipeline: a checker that rejects broken programs before any IR is built, then a typed netlist IR, then several backends. It can print truth tables (useful for someone who had to memorise them for an electronics degree), Mermaid dataflow diagrams, VCD waveforms, and Verilog that gets checked for validity.
- The example was a half adder: sum = A XOR B, carry = A AND B, written as typed bits instead of Verilog =assign= statements. Conditions map to multiplexers.
- Limitations: one module and one file at a time, no arrays or loops, signed values only, bits and booleans only ("arithmetic with extra steps"), and a hard-coded parser. It can handle basic sequential and combinational logic with clocks.
- Lessons: he tried LLVM and then MLIR and found both far too big for this. RustHDL is the larger cousin he'd like to catch up with. The main takeaway was not to get overconfident when you're up against decades of accumulated work.

In Q&A someone asked how Frag compares to Spade. He said Frag "can't even walk yet". He also confirmed that the Verilog backend outputs expressions without simplifying them. The repo is =afnanksalal/frag=.
