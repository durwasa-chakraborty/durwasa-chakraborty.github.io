> *"Before you start any work, ask yourself three questions ::
> Why am I doing it? What might the results be? And am I capable of
> succeeding at it? Only when you have thought deeply and found
> satisfactory answers, go ahead."*
> attributed to Chanakya, and quoted to me by
> {{< mention "Anup Mishra" "https://www.linkedin.com/in/anupm5999/" >}}

> This piece is mine :: the notes, the confessions, the self-diagnosis. I used
> an LLM to fix my spelling and to help me turn a wall of run-on sentences
> into paragraphs a stranger could survive.

Ideally I should have asked the question in the title a long, long time ago.
Around 2023, say, while I was still packing for a grand exit but here we are.

Here is the ledger, since a ledger is the honest way to open this. On one side:
a paycheck with a comma in a pleasant place, an apartment in downtown Seattle
and then in Burnaby, a porch, and a piano that arrived under the listing's
casual heading of *well furnished* and a nice red cherry Nissan Rogue in the parking spot. On the
other side: a room in the inner wing of Mahanadi hostel, which on a bad
afternoon does a convincing impression of the floor of a thermal power plant,
and a Rockrider cycle. 

I signed that deal voluntarily to come to the other side, and this is not a man
laying out his sacrifices for somebody else to admire. Nobody made me do any of
it. I traded the money for intellectual freedom because I wanted to, by my own
volition, and I own the trade. Then I got into the PhD program, sat my
comprehensive exam, performed badly, sat it again, performed badly again, and
the question that should have been asked while the boxes were still open came
and sat on my chest at 2 a.m.

**Am I fit for a PhD?**

---

## Industry lets you reason lazily

Amazon has a leadership principle called *Dive Deep*. I can recite it along with
other LPs. I can also tell you that I mostly did not do it, and that the system
around me was engineered so that not doing it was the correct professional
choice.

A service is down. Is it mine? No? Then I do not need to know *why*. That is not
negligence, that is the org chart working as designed. It is abstraction,
computer science's single greatest gift, aimed at people instead of at code. You
are handed an interface, a runbook, an escalation path, and a firefighter access
credential that expires in four hours; and the whole apparatus is tuned to
convert a question into a ticket before the question can become a curiosity.

And where is the time? - Like, genuinely.  There is on-call. There is the escalation.
There is the postmortem for the escalation, and the action items from the
postmortem, and the sprint that absorbs the action items. Depth is not forbidden
in industry. It is simply never the thing on fire.

Which is not to say it never happens. When it does happen it goes further than
any seminar room I have ever sat in. Put
a price on a *why* and watch the company fund it without blinking. If your
binary answers in eight milliseconds and the competitor's answers in four,
you will be handed a quarter
and a mandate to go find them. And you will go *down* - through the code to the
the exact electron responsible for flipping that one bit sitting exactly where 
the datasheet wants it.

That is *Dive Deep* in its fullest sense, and it is a beautiful thing to watch.
But notice what licensed it. The question was never "why does this happen." The
question was "can we extract money from knowing why this happens", and wherever
the answer is no, the question gets closed before anybody thinks to ask it.
Curiosity in industry is not banned. It is capitalised. And why not :: it is
somebody else's money. Nobody is paying you to feel intellectually alive at
eleven in the morning ; they are paying you to do a job, and that arrangement
is written down in the offer letter in a font everybody can read. I have no
complaint against it, and anyone who takes the salary and then sneers at the
terms is being professionally dishonest.

The complaint is against myself. The strange part is that back then, during my work in the industry, 
I was confident I would make a good PhD
student. My reasoning was: I am curious about everything, and everything
deterministic has an explanation waiting underneath it, and I am the sort of
person who wants the explanation.

## A detour through God, and arrogance

I am a religious person, and I have never been able to make atheism sit still in
my head as a purely scientific position.

Osho (Btw! he did his degree from  {{< mention "Jabalpur" "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabalpur" >}},
) says - 

> *"Belief is a barrier. Trust is a bridge."*
> Osho, *From Death to Deathlessness*, chapter 3, 4 August 1985

Everyone adores him for lines like that. Richard Dawkins puts the same instinct more arithmetically
{{< mention "in conversation" "https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U0Xn60Zw03A" >}}
:: the probability of God is on the order of the probability of leprechauns or
werewolves. Non-zero. Just very, very unlikely.

I have never been able to shake the feeling that a good part of atheism runs on
something else. Not evidence (or the lack of it while bringing in _belief_), but posture: *we don't know yet, but we will know
eventually; we always have.* That sentence is doing enormous unpaid work. It
promises a future receipt for a present certainty. It is, I think, a kind of
arrogance ; the same arrogance that let me believe curiosity alone would carry
me through a doctorate. I will understand everything. I will find the answer to
everything. I only need to want it badly enough.

Comprehensive exam, attempt II, disagreed.

## The roof of the hostel room

I want to be careful here,I dont want a reader to read this like self-pity.
It is not. This is the conclusion of several hours spent lying on a hostel bed
staring at the ceiling, trying to hold two things apart that look identical from
the inside: the sunk cost fallacy, and a legitimate early exit. Sunk cost tells
you to stay because of what you have already burned; the early exit tells you to
leave because the remaining road does not lead where you thought. They give
opposite advice and at 2 a.m. the only way to tell
them apart is to be brutally specific about what is actually broken.

So, specifically. I know a great many technologies. I know the *whats*, though superficially. Give me
a system and I will tell you what it does, what it is called, what it competes
with, and which conference talk to watch. Where I have hit a wall is the *whys*,
and the wall is made of memory.

I forget. I forget constantly. I cannot hold last semester's material without a
lookup, let alone the computer science I read a decade ago. I cannot, sitting
here, reconstruct from first principles on some of the whys that a PhD student must absolutely know. The mechanism underneath, the actual reason, has to be
re-derived every single time, as though I had never learnt it.

A good response to this chronic forgetfullness was the most useful
sentence: **internalise the whys**. The whys do
not consume memory bandwidth. A *what* is a fact you have to store. A *why* is a
generator that regenerates the facts on demand. I had spent a decade filling a
cache and never once building the function.

## Half a decade of easy lookup

My hypothesis, and it is only a hypothesis, is that the lookup did this to me.

Want a query, Google it. Want a shell incantation, AskUbuntu-it. Want a snippet,
Stack Overflow it. Want to know why a particular driver refuses to install, dig up
some fossilised IRC archive from 2009 and read a stranger's answer from a
lifetime ago. Every one of these worked. Every one of these shipped. And every
one of these quietly taught my brain that retention was somebody else's
responsibility, until the brain took the hint and became a volatile resource ::
correct while powered, empty on reboot.

I was never a brilliant student anyway. My last great academic performance was
in Class X, 17 years ago - my last big achievement will become an adult next
year. Nothing in my academic record has ever screamed genius, and I have made my
peace with the record. But I was curious, and I am curious, and I expect I will
die curious.

Which brings the uncomfortable question :: **is curiosity satiated by a PhD?**

No. Honestly, no. Curiosity is satisfied by an afternoon and a good enough
search engine. Nobody needs six years and a committee for curiosity.

So if the stated reason for coming here is not the real reason, the decision
deserves to be re-audited from the top. Which is where Anup's three questions
come in.

## Why am I doing it?

I want to teach. That is the half of it.

Not "I want a faculty position", though that is the shape it takes on paper.
I want to be the person in the room who has patience, and who knows the whats,
the whys, the hows, and the several different roads that arrive at each of them,
because a student never comes at a concept from the road you planned for.

The other half is that I want the other thing academia has that industry structurally cannot give
me :: the right to spend six months on something whose utility is laughable.

Here is the concrete daydream. I notice a Software Engineering problem.
The clean fix is not a patch, it is a small language ; a DSL, a
compiler pass, a language-level expression. In industry I know exactly what
happens next, because I have watched it happen. A senior smiles at me,
kindly, and says no. Not because the idea is wrong, but because the time
investment is bizarre against the return, and they are right to say it. The
economics does not checkout.

You can only build the beautiful (maybe useless) thing when that kind of money is not on the table.


## Am I capable of what is required?

Right now, every bone in my body says **no**.

I have some cold comfort available :: capability is usually a function of time,
and with time everything changes. But I do not have age on my side the way my
classmates do, and the honest reading of the evidence is that capability, past a
point, is largely cemented - neuroplasticity or some cool biomechanical word which appears on these dopamine addiction videos.

The capability part has a resounding no in the present tense, and the entire bet of the
next few years is whether a sufficiently strong *why* can drag a mediocre
*capable* through the trials and tribulations of the whole journey.

That is the whole gamble, stated plainly. It is a worse gamble than the one I
thought I was making in 2023, when I believed curiosity was a qualification.

__I am taking it anyway.__

Hopefully, same time next year, this page has some answers on it that I actually
earned. And if it does not, at least it will have the ledger, honestly kept, and
a record of a man who could still tell the difference between a sunk cost and a
road that ran out.
