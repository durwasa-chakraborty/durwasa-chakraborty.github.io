In 2014, while I was doing my BE at Jabalpur Engineering College, my senior
[[TODO-linkedin-url][Mayank Kumar Sharma]] said we should be going to conferences like PyCon,
which was happening in Bangalore at the NIMHANS Convention Centre. Back then going to
Bangalore was royalty. There was exactly one train. And to be honest, Mayank Sir's idea
was simply to go and see what else was running in the world apart from /Let Us C/ by
Yashavant Kanetkar.

I did not know anything about Python. I had installed the Python REPL a week before. But it
was a great experience.

Let me hang some really dirty clothes out to dry. Before PyCon, my idea was that competitive
coding was the only way to make a career in software engineering. If you finished 9000th in
the CodeChef monthly long challenge, you basically sucked. Please go write GATE, CAT, UPSC or
Bank PO, or hope that Infosys Mysore takes you in, puts you up in an exotic hostel and runs
you through six months of disaster management.

Fair. Sounds like a good deal. But what is open source? I remember opening a codebase in C,
because C was the only thing I knew back then. It was Mozilla Firefox, the Gecko project, and
I swear to the Almighty (and I am a very theist guy) that code did not look like C.

So Mayank Sir said: explore the world. There are other levels of abstraction. Software
engineering is a very different thing from competitive coding.

Great. So Vipul Sharma and I took a train from Jabalpur to Bangalore. I am still bemused that
the first time we went to Bangalore we had to get down at Kundalahalli (which, for the
uninitiated, is very different from Kundalahalli Gate) and stay at Apoorv Anand Sir's
apartment. He was working at $\mu\sigma$ back then.

Fast forward twelve years. Same venue, NIMHANS. Still hoping to give a talk at PyCon or some
language conference someday, but this time I started with IndiaFOSS. Day one, I run into
Apoorv Anand Sir, and the circle closes nicely.

So here is a blog about my time in Bangalore at IndiaFOSS and some of the interesting talks I
attended.

** Semantic Quantization in KV Cache Eviction

The first talk, as per my notes. GiGi described an experiment in evicting KV cache entries.
The motivation is running open models on a laptop. Laptops are small, and without an efficient
KV cache you will hit OOM. There are well-known eviction policies like H2O and SnapKV, which
probably deserve a blog post of their own.

The idea is to score tokens using only the key and value tensors already sitting in the cache.
Whenever I hear the word "tensor", my ability to visualise the engineering design takes a hit.
But I could follow up to that point, and especially the overall theme, which was JPEG: you
lose the fine detail but keep what the image means.

Glad I attended. I learned a few cool words like PyTorch SDPA and PagedAttention, both of
which are now on my reading list.

** Bend, Don't Break: Strongly Typed Functional Programming

The next talk was by Anupam Jain, and this was more up my alley because it was about strongly
typed functional programming. I could see some strong head nods in the audience. Haskell
folks, I tell you!

It was a good introduction to what a "rich type system" even means. Anyone with industry
experience has this complaint: if I change type =x= to =xButCooler=, I have to change it
everywhere, and that is not cool. There were some jabs at Rust here and there, but overall the
talk was very informative. He used the classic division example (ask for a =NonZeroInt=
instead of returning a =Maybe=) to show how types turn the argument around. It is a different
way of doing the same thing, and the whole game of languages is being able to say precisely
what you want to do.

A very nice video if you are getting started with functional programming and types. It is
timestamped, so [[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rb1ZALRGFjw&t=1037s][please watch it here]].

** Databases in Ethereum Nodes

Obviously ETH is still a huge interest of mine. It is Turing complete, and it is also the
reason I fill in a different tax document every year for owning a very insignificant amount
of ETH.

Sudeep (Erigon) explained how Erigon stores Ethereum data and why it uses a custom storage
engine instead of an off-the-shelf database. An Erigon archive node takes about 8 TB, against
roughly 20 TB for less efficient clients, and it syncs in hours rather than days. That makes
it the usual choice for RPC providers and analytics.

The detail that caught my attention was the monotonically increasing transaction number.
Ethereum's spec points to a moment in history with a pair, (block number, transaction index
within the block), which is cumbersome to work with. Erigon flattens that pair into a single
integer that only ever goes up across blocks. That sounds like bookkeeping, but it is what
makes the rest work. To find "the balance of this account three years ago", you keep, for each
account, the sorted list of txNums at which it changed, and look up the first change after
your query point. A sorted list of increasing integers is about the most compressible thing
there is, so Erigon stores it with an Elias–Fano-style encoding that is close to optimal. One
boring integer turns a temporal query into a successor search. As someone who just wrote a
whole post about invariants, I have to love an invariant that pays rent.

** PeerDAS: Erasure Coding When You Can't Trust the Storage

Some Reed–Solomon-style coding, presented by someone from Nimbus, the Ethereum consensus
client written in Nim. Understanding the bit parity was way above my pay grade. I did get the
opening: scratched CDs and half-torn UPI QR codes still work, and so should a peer-to-peer
network where peers lie or hide data.

Honestly, both ETH talks went over my head, the way a cover drive goes over a fielder who was
busy checking his phone. But "how do you store the history of a world computer" and "how do
you know the data exists without downloading all of it" are exactly the two questions I want
to go and read about. Consider them bookmarked.

** Benchmarking Can Be Hard, Here's Why We're Still Creating a New One for OCaml

Then came the star talk, and the first from FP Launchpad. It was like Horatio's introduction
in /Hamlet/: the friend who shows up in the first scene and sets the tone for everything after.

Navaneeth (FP Launchpad, IIT Madras) gave the talk in two halves: why benchmarking is hard,
and what the OCaml community is building to deal with it.

For the first half he used a long-running Java PDF pipeline. Run it several times and the
numbers drift: JIT warm-up, GC behaviour, P-cores versus E-cores, thermal and power
throttling, ASLR, background updates, even the season (February runs cooler than July). Some
noise is periodic and some is random. His points: performance is a distribution, not a
number. Control what you can and collect evidence for the rest. Sometimes you throw
everything out and start again.

The second half covered OCaml 5, which added multicore parallelism and effect-based
concurrency. The old benchmark suite had drifted away from real workloads. The compiler and
runtime got tuned for the benchmarks, while production code, Jane Street's adoption of OCaml 5
for example, did not get faster.

The payoff is upstreaming GC and compaction work with confidence. In the Q&A he mentioned his
own automatic-compaction PR. The tooling showed that his machine was the problem, it exposed
alignment effects that he fixed with compiler flags, and it showed that compaction causes no
slowdown beyond the error bounds.

** What LLVM Does Differently When the Target Is CPU vs GPU

Bhuvan (Point Blank, ACM SIGPLAN mentee) walked through what LLVM does differently when the
target is a GPU. He started with a plain =if= on =threadIdx=. On a CPU each thread takes one
branch. On a GPU a warp (32 threads, or a wavefront on AMD) executes in lockstep, so a
divergent branch effectively runs both sides, one after the other.

Anyone taking a GPU course at undergrad or grad level /must/ hear this talk. It assumes you
know what a pass is, but beyond that there is a lot of information packed into 15 minutes.

** PythonBPF: Now with Loops, Custom Funcs, and More Maps!

Pragyansh (FP Launchpad, Canonical) and Varun (IIT Roorkee, BPF kernel contributor) gave an
update on PythonBPF, which compiles ordinary decorated Python to eBPF through LLVM IR. They
compared it with the alternatives: restricted C, BCC (awkward inline C), Aya (needs Rust,
slow for quick experiments) and bpftrace (yet another DSL to learn). The pitch is that BPF
work is mostly about producing data, and Python is where you end up analysing and plotting it
anyway, with Jupyter and LSP support for free. The pipeline builds a Python =vmlinux= module
from bpftool's C header, picks out the BPF chunks, runs allocation passes and emits LLVM IR for
the BPF backend.

This was definitely my favourite talk. It has FOSS smeared all over it: the modus operandi and
even the way things were discussed felt very SIG-like (as the k8s folks would say). The demo
was Snake running inside the kernel, "the only snake game that needs sudo". Of course, I am
also trying to stay in the good books of a Canonical developer who shares my space-time
continuum (he literally sits next to the lab), but credit where it is due: great presentation
skills by Varun.

** One Language, All GPU: How Julia Targets Different GPU Backends from a Single Kernel

A beautiful talk by Shreyas Hegde, who did GSoC with Julia. I remember the Julia folks from MIT
holding nice community gatherings in Boston cafés. I have worked at MATLAB, and Julia is also a
language for the scientific community. But this talk too was very much in the spirit of FOSS.

Shreyas (JuliaGPU, GSoC contributor, compiler research intern at IIT Kanpur) explained how one
Julia kernel compiles to CUDA, AMD ROCm, Intel oneAPI and Apple Metal. The framing was a firm
that cannot afford Nvidia and an intern called "Dumbo" who assumes one programming model covers
every GPU. JuliaGPU tries to make that assumption hold with close to vendor-level performance.

The stack:
- *AcceleratedKernels.jl*: portable parallel algorithms (sort, reduce, mapreduce, accumulate,
  foreachindex) with target-specific tuning. Most of his GSoC work went here. He walked through
  accumulate (a prefix scan): a local-memory scan per work group, then a block-level scan, then
  a final pass.
- *KernelInterface*: device facts such as the maximum work-group size (1024 on CUDA, 256 on
  Metal). This was the bit I (a) could follow along with, (b) found made so much sense, and
  (c) think would make a very good LLVM poster. To quote Shreyas: "portable does not mean
  hardware-blind".
- *GPUCompiler.jl*: lowers Julia typed IR to LLVM IR, and from there to PTX, AMDGCN/HSA or
  SPIR-V.
- *Backend packages* (CUDA.jl, AMDGPU.jl, oneAPI.jl, Metal.jl) handle what cannot be
  abstracted, such as native memory fences.

A very nice and neat design. For first-year students who want to get into a programming
language and see what a community builds, I would highly suggest the Julia community. The
community is very nice, and it makes sense to get involved with a language that has stronger
types and stronger principles, where feature requests are not driven by popularity but by a
real scientific pull.

It also seems that squeezing out optimisation by making a language GPU-friendly might be how
the next generation of languages gets designed. That is really an argument in favour of GPU
lowering and LLVM, and I do not know that field well enough to push it. It feels like we are in
a dot-com-bubble moment where FOMO decides what everyone learns. Nonetheless, as a language
enthusiast, it is more academically rewarding to be part of Julia, and well, OCaml, than, say,
JavaScript or Python or... C.

* So what was my talk about?

It was about program verification. The title was /LGTM Is Not a Theorem: Pragmatic
Verification and the Economics of Software Confidence/, and the [[https://anachronisticmonk.github.io/indiafoss/deck.html][slides are here]].

The talk followed a single refund function through a bad week. On Tuesday we refund only
charges that have =SETTLED=. On Wednesday a ticket comes in: same-day cancellations are still
=CAPTURED=, so let's refund those too. You copy the test, change one word, and the build goes
green at 100% coverage. Ship it. Thursday, 02:14, Sev-2: the gateway times out, the job
retries, and the customer gets paid twice. So you add an =if charge.refunded: return= guard,
add a test for it, coverage is back to 100%, and the incident is closed. Properly fixed.

Except it is not. Two workers can both read =refunded = false=, both call the gateway, and both
set the flag afterwards. No timeout needed, and no test will catch it, because a test has to
pick one interleaving and there are exponentially many. So stop guessing the schedule. Write
down one sentence that must stay true, "the flag and the money agree" (at most one payout, and
no payout while the flag is down), and prove that every step preserves it. The moment you try
that in Lean, the proof obligation for "gateway returned, flag still false" turns into
=1 = 0=. The goal itself hands you the bug, along with every other route to it that you never
imagined. The fix is a compare-and-swap, which in Django is one atomic
=filter(refunded=False).update(refunded=True)= that reads and writes the flag in the same move.
The Lean model gets the same single step, and the theorem goes through for any number of
workers in any order.

The second example was a handful of lines in Django's =Paginator=. I mutated them nine ways
(off-by-ones, flipped comparisons, a dropped =orphans=) and the suite caught every one. The
suite is good. Then I read the rest of the file: Django had shipped for years without anyone
writing down that =orphans= must be smaller than =per_page=. =Paginator(rows, per_page=2,
orphans=20)= happily gave you one page of 22 rows, and the =ValueError= only arrives in Django
7.0. In Lean, the theorem "every page has fewer than =2 * per_page= rows" builds fine with the
hypothesis =o < k=. Delete that hypothesis and =omega= hands you the counterexample. The
precondition Django found the hard way falls straight out of the proof attempt.

Then the honest part: if the maths is this good, why are any of us still writing tests?
Because software has economics. You spend something, you get something, and it has to pay for
itself. My argument was that the price has moved. The steep part used to be /producing/ the
proof, and that is increasingly something you can hand over to a machine, because a checked
proof is a proof no matter who or what wrote it. That is not true of generated code. What is
left for us is coming up with the theorem, reading the statement, and deciding whether it says
enough. Start at the boundary, where your state meets something you do not control: a gateway
that retries, a client that parses your JSON, a queue that delivers twice. And you do not even
have to start with a proof. =pip install hypothesis= makes you write the contract down without
making you discharge it, which gets you most of the value for a fraction of the price, this
afternoon.

LGTM is a vote. A theorem is a receipt.

** What I actually learned

I used to have every question that someone without a verification background has today. Isn't
this what tests are for? We have 100% coverage, code review, staging, canaries. Who writes
proofs for a refund function? Isn't this something for aerospace and academics with too much
time on their hands?

Five years in the industry, at IBM, MathWorks and AWS, gave me the material to answer those
questions. I just did not know it at the time. I have been the person who copied the test and
changed one word. I have been on the call at 2 a.m. where every dashboard was green and the
money was still leaving. I have reviewed pull requests, typed LGTM, and meant "I could not
find anything wrong", which is a very different statement from "nothing is wrong". Coverage
tells you which lines ran, not which states are reachable. Tests sample the behaviour of a
program. They do not characterise it. Every postmortem I sat through was, in hindsight, a
missing invariant that nobody had written down.

That experience is exactly what makes the case for verification click for me. When I see an
invariant, I do not see a Greek letter; I see the Sev-2 that it would have prevented. And when
I see a proof attempt fail, I read the stuck goal the way I used to read a stack trace.
Industry teaches you /where/ software breaks: at the boundaries, under retries, under
concurrency, at the edge of a precondition nobody wrote down. Verification gives you a
language to say /why/ it cannot break there.

The bigger realisation, now that I am on the PhD side, is that doing serious programming
languages work means generating proofs, or at least having some semblance of one. You cannot
claim a type system is sound, an optimisation is correct, or a transformation preserves
meaning because it passed the examples you tried. "It worked on my examples" is the PL
equivalent of LGTM. The field asks for receipts, and I have come to think the rest of software
engineering should too, at least at the boundaries where it matters and now that the receipts
have become cheap.
